# Introduction

Beta-blocker therapy after myocardial infarction (MI) has been a cornerstone of secondary prevention, though most supporting trials were conducted in the pre-reperfusion era among patients with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF).[1-3] Benefit in contemporary patients with preserved or mildly reduced ejection fraction remains uncertain.[4,5]

## Recent Meta-Analyses Suggest a Sharp Threshold Effect

In 2025, two companion individual patient data (IPD) meta-analyses from the same research group were published in *The Lancet* and *New England Journal of Medicine*, analyzing contemporary trials (REBOOT, BETAMI, DANBLOCK, CAPITAL-RCT).[8,9] These analyses reported strikingly different findings by LVEF:

- **EF 40-49% (N=1,885):** HR 0.75 (95% CI 0.58-0.97, p=0.031) for death, MI, or heart failure[8]
- **EF ≥50% (N=17,801):** HR 0.97 (95% CI 0.87-1.07, p=0.54)[9]

Based on these findings, the investigators concluded a sharp efficacy threshold exists at LVEF=50%, prompting calls for ejection fraction-stratified treatment guidelines with potential implications for millions of patients worldwide.[10-12]

## The Challenge: Biological Implausibility and Statistical Overfitting

This claimed threshold is difficult to reconcile with known biology. Beta-blocker mechanisms—heart rate reduction, anti-arrhythmic effects, neurohormonal modulation—would be expected to vary gradually, not abruptly, across the ejection fraction spectrum.[13,14] Moreover, ejection fraction is a continuous physiological variable with test-retest variability of 5-10%.[16,17] The notion that treatment efficacy pivots on a single percentage point within measurement error raises fundamental questions about statistical robustness.

The divergent findings exemplify a pervasive methodological challenge: spurious subgroup effects from data-dependent threshold selection.[19-21] When continuous variables are dichotomized, investigators can test multiple cutpoints until a "significant" result emerges, capitalizing on random variation.[22,23] Statistical hallmarks of such overfitting include non-significant interaction tests, underpowered analyses, extreme statistical fragility, and failure to validate in held-out data.[26,27] Despite these known risks, formal validation procedures are rarely performed in published subgroup analyses.[28,29]

## Study Objectives

We had three objectives:

1. **To evaluate the statistical robustness** of the claimed EF=50% threshold using published data, including formal interaction testing, power analysis, and fragility assessment.

2. **To quantify the false-positive rate** of spurious threshold detection through simulation studies matching the original trial structure.

3. **To propose a validation framework** for future subgroup claims from IPD meta-analyses.

We hypothesized the EF=50% threshold might not meet standard validation criteria and that simulations would demonstrate high false-positive rates when continuous variables are dichotomized without proper validation.
